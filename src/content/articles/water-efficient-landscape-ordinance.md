Most people who have heard of California's water ordinance have heard one
number: five hundred square feet. It is a real threshold and it is often quoted
at homeowners who do not actually fall under it.

There are two thresholds, and which one applies depends on whether the garden
is new or already there.

## The two thresholds

**A new landscape of 500 square feet or more** falls under the ordinance. This
is the number everyone repeats, and for new development it is correct.

**An existing landscape being rebuilt** is held to a different figure. That
threshold is 2,500 square feet, and it did not change when the state tightened
the rules for new work in 2015. Before then, new landscapes were held to
somewhere between 2,500 and 5,000 square feet depending on the project.

So a homeowner redoing a courtyard, a side garden or a single bed in an
established property is very often below the line, while the same area planted
at a newly built house is not.

## The condition people skip

Both thresholds carry the same qualifier, and it does more work than the
numbers do. The ordinance reaches projects that **require a permit, a plan
check or a design review**. A rehabilitated landscape is subject to it only
when it is tied to a building or landscape permit, a plan check or a design
review.

That is why two projects of the same size can be treated completely
differently. It is the permit that pulls the ordinance in, not the square
footage on its own.

## What compliance actually involves

For projects that do fall under it, the state provides two routes.

The **prescriptive route** is a checklist. It is available for smaller
projects, and it is the lighter path: meet the specified requirements and you
are compliant without modelling anything.

The **performance route** uses a water budget. A landscape documentation
package sets out planting, irrigation and grading, and the calculations
demonstrate the design stays inside the water allowance for the site. This is
where hydrozones come in: grouping plants so that things with the same thirst
share a valve, rather than watering a lawn and a stand of natives on the same
schedule.

Either way, the project closes with a **certificate of completion**, signed and
submitted, confirming what was installed matches what was approved. It is easy
to forget at the end of a build and awkward to produce months later.

## Your town may have its own version

The state ordinance is a model. Cities and counties could adopt it as written,
or adopt their own so long as theirs conserves at least as much water. Where a
local agency did neither, the state version took effect by default.

Atherton, for example, replaced its own water-efficient landscaping chapter and
adopted the state ordinance by reference, with revisions, in December 2019. Its
building department publishes the worksheets, the prescriptive option and the
certificate of completion alongside its other submittal checklists.

The practical consequence is that the rule is a state rule and the counter you
submit to is a local one. The forms, the reviewer and the turnaround are your
town's.

## What this means before you start

If the project is a new garden at a new house, assume the ordinance applies and
budget the documentation into the design rather than discovering it at plan
check.

If the project is an existing garden being rebuilt, the size question is worth
answering early and honestly, because the answer changes what the design has to
carry.

And in either case, the thing that most often causes a delay is not the
ordinance itself. It is submitting a planting and irrigation plan that was
drawn without the water budget in mind, and having to redraw it.
